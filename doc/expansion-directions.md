# Element matching should lead onionskin's expansion

This report asks where onionskin should go next. onionskin is a Clojure and ClojureScript library for testing the layout and style of web pages. It loads a page in a headless Chrome, Chromium, Edge or Firefox and captures the box and the computed style of every element. A test then checks the capture against a spec of relations between elements, e.g. that the logo is left of the search field. A diff of two captures shows what a change to the CSS did, or that it did nothing.

The library has three limits today, and each one points in a direction that it could expand in:

1. **More states of a page.** A capture shows the page at one viewport width, in the light colour scheme, with nothing hovered or focused. This direction adds forced `:hover`, `:focus` and `:active` states, dark mode and other user preferences, other default font sizes, and facts that hold over a range of widths.
2. **Checks that need no spec.** Every expectation has to be written by hand. This direction adds checks that find likely defects on their own: sideways scrolling, elements that stick out of the viewport or of their container, clipped text, colliding elements and unintended wrapping.
3. **Diffs that survive changes to the markup.** A diff pairs the elements of two captures by their place in the document, so one new wrapper element breaks the pairing of everything inside it. This direction matches elements across such changes. It also covers filters for expected changes, labels for style values that depend on layout, and comparisons between browser engines.

For each direction, the report sets out what research, tools and specifications establish, and what onionskin should take from them or avoid. The first two sections cover direction 1: states and preferences, then ranges of widths. The third covers direction 2. The fourth and fifth cover direction 3: matching elements, then filters, labels and engines. The report ends in design choices and an order of work. It follows on from the research behind onionskin's current design ([prior-art.md](prior-art.md)) and numbers its design choices on from that report's twenty.

**The answer in short.** onionskin should expand in all three directions, in a fixed order: repair element matching in diffs first, then capture more states and ranges of widths, then add a short set of spec-free checks, and compare browser engines last. The order follows from the evidence. Diffs pair elements by absolute path today, and absolute paths are the weakest signal in every locator study: they lost 67% of elements across one release and 79% across one to five years, and one added wrapper already hid a real colour change in the only comparable tool that documented it. A GumTree-style matcher that pairs identical subtrees first is small, needs no dependency and can be tested without a browser. Capturing more states has the best ratio of gain to effort in Chrome, where the DevTools Protocol forces 28 pseudo-classes, overrides seven media features and sets the default font size on a loaded page; Firefox's WebDriver BiDi offers only `prefers-color-scheme` (since Firefox 159) and real input, so other preferences cost a relaunch. Width ranges have the strongest numbers: checks at six fixed widths missed 9 of 48 seeded regressions that range-tagged facts caught, and tool preset widths would miss 19% to 34% of real failures, yet the method has had no maintained implementation since 2018. Spec-free checks come after ranges because their record without a cross-width comparison is poor: about 71% of reported box overlaps and protrusions were invisible to people, and only checks on text and on the viewport held up. Across engines, the browser vendors' own shared tests accept layout differences under 1 px, so relations among chosen elements are the claim that can hold. Three gaps limit every recommendation: no study measures tree matching on real before-and-after pages, none measures a DOM-based rule for clipped text on the web, and none counts how often current engines lay out real pages differently.

**How sources are labelled.** Peer-reviewed papers are cited by author and year, and the reference list gives authors, title, venue and a DOI or URL for each. Preprints, theses, specifications, browser source code, tool documentation and grey literature are named as such where they carry a claim. Anything that was not confirmed from a primary source is marked in the text. Some findings come from local tests made for this report against headless Chrome 157 and Firefox 160, through onionskin's own drivers. These are single measurements on one machine. They are marked wherever they appear, and they are not published findings. Counts that were computed for this report from published tables or from source files are marked as computed. Tool maintenance data was checked on 2026-11-12. Where the report needs something from the earlier research, it restates it.

## Chrome forces 28 pseudo-classes per page; Firefox needs real input and relaunches

A capture of the resting, light-mode page at one width leaves most of a style sheet unexercised. Two surveys of live sites (grey literature, not peer-reviewed) show how common state rules are. Project Wallace's 2026 analysis of the home pages of over 100,000 sites found `:hover` on 95.2% of sites, `:focus` on 88.6%, `:active` on 83.9% and `prefers-reduced-motion` on 44.2% ([Project Wallace 2026](https://www.projectwallace.com/the-css-selection/2026)). The HTTP Archive's Web Almanac 2025 found a `:focus` selector on about 90% of pages and `prefers-color-scheme` on about 13% ([Web Almanac 2025](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/accessibility)). The two disagree on `prefers-color-scheme` (5.1% against about 13%) and on `:focus-visible` (48.2% against about 25%), because samples and analysers differ, and neither reports what share of rules, as opposed to sites, depends on a state. **No peer-reviewed study was found that measures which interaction states web visual-regression tools miss, or that studies dark-mode defects on web pages.** The solid research is on Android: of 1,074 setting-related defects in 180 apps, about 70.7% caused failures other than crashes, which ordinary testing tools cannot detect for lack of an oracle ([Sun et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3460319.3464806)), and dark mode has its own study of more than 6,000 apps ([Ma et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3604607)). On the web, the earlier evidence still applies: CILLA found 60% of selectors unused across crawled states ([Mesbah and Mirshokraie 2012](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE.2012.6227174)), and Mazinanian and colleagues named unvisited states as the main threat to their refactoring checks ([Mazinanian et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2635868.2635879)).

### What each protocol offers

Chrome's DevTools Protocol covers the whole need on a loaded page without a restart. `CSS.forcePseudoState` makes a node match the listed pseudo-classes whenever its style is computed, and Chromium's source accepts **28 names**, among them hover, focus, focus-visible, focus-within, active, visited, checked, disabled and target ([CDP CSS domain](https://github.com/ChromeDevTools/devtools-protocol/blob/master/pdl/domains/CSS.pdl); [Chromium inspector_css_agent.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/inspector/inspector_css_agent.cc)). `Emulation.setEmulatedMedia` is marked stable and overrides the media type and exactly seven media features: prefers-color-scheme, prefers-contrast, prefers-reduced-motion, prefers-reduced-data, prefers-reduced-transparency, forced-colors and color-gamut. Each call replaces the earlier list instead of merging with it ([CDP Emulation domain](https://github.com/ChromeDevTools/devtools-protocol/blob/master/pdl/domains/Emulation.pdl); [Chromium media_feature_overrides.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/css/media_feature_overrides.cc); [Chromium inspector_emulation_agent.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/inspector/inspector_emulation_agent.cc)). `Page.setFontSizes` sets the default font size ([CDP Page domain](https://github.com/ChromeDevTools/devtools-protocol/blob/master/pdl/domains/Page.pdl)). The catch is status: the whole CSS domain and `Page.setFontSizes` are marked experimental, and no statement was found on how long that will last.

The source code shows three traps. Forcing `focus` on a node also makes its ancestors match `:focus-within`, but forcing `hover` has no such step, so a rule such as `.card:hover .title` is exercised only when the state is forced on `.card` itself; real hover always covers the ancestor chain ([Chromium inspector_css_agent.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/inspector/inspector_css_agent.cc); [Selectors Level 4, :hover](https://drafts.csswg.org/selectors-4/#the-hover-pseudo)). Page script never sees `:visited` styles: the inspector builds its computed style with a flag that everything else leaves off, so only `CSS.getComputedStyleForNode` returns the visited colour ([Chromium css_computed_style_declaration.h](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/css/css_computed_style_declaration.h)). Since `:visited` may only change a short list of colour properties, it cannot change layout ([MDN, Privacy and the :visited selector](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Selectors/Privacy_and_:visited)). And `Emulation.setAutoDarkModeOverride`, `setEmulatedVisionDeficiency` and `setDefaultBackgroundColorOverride` do not go through style resolution, so they are of no use to a tool that reads boxes and computed styles.

Firefox is far behind. The WebDriver BiDi Editor's Draft defines `emulation.setMediaFeaturesOverride` with a map of 29 media features, and requires a browser to answer "unsupported operation" for any it cannot override ([WebDriver BiDi, setMediaFeaturesOverride](https://w3c.github.io/webdriver-bidi/#command-emulation-setMediaFeaturesOverride)). Firefox's own module supports one entry of that map, `prefers-color-scheme`, which landed for **Firefox 159 (27 October 2026)**; the umbrella bug for the rest is open ([Firefox emulation.sys.mjs](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/remote/webdriver-bidi/modules/root/emulation.sys.mjs); [Bugzilla 2064354](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2064354); [Bugzilla 2064352](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2064352)). A text search of the specification source finds no command for pseudo-class state, page zoom, text zoom or default font size ([WebDriver BiDi source](https://github.com/w3c/webdriver-bidi/blob/main/index.bs)). What BiDi does have is `input.performActions`, which sends real pointer and key input ([WebDriver BiDi, input.performActions](https://w3c.github.io/webdriver-bidi/#command-input-performActions)). Firefox holds per-page switches for reduced motion, forced colours, contrast, zoom and pseudo-class locks, but only for privileged code; starting Firefox with `MOZ_REMOTE_ALLOW_SYSTEM_ACCESS=1` opens that route, Mozilla says to use it only when really needed, and whether content elements can be reached that way was not confirmed ([Firefox BrowsingContext.webidl](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/dom/chrome-webidl/BrowsingContext.webidl); [Firefox Remote Protocols, Security](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/remote/Security.html)). Playwright avoids the problem by shipping a patched Firefox that understands its own protocol ([Playwright ffPage.ts](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright/blob/main/packages/playwright-core/src/server/firefox/ffPage.ts)). Everything else needs a preference written into the profile before launch, so each combination costs a browser start.

| Need | Chrome (CDP) | Firefox (BiDi and preferences) |
|---|---|---|
| Forced `:hover`, `:focus`, `:active` | `CSS.forcePseudoState`, per node, experimental | no command; privileged `addPseudoClassLock` only (unconfirmed) |
| Real hover, press, Tab focus | `Input.dispatchMouseEvent`, `dispatchKeyEvent`, stable | `input.performActions` |
| Colour scheme | `Emulation.setEmulatedMedia`, per page | `emulation.setMediaFeaturesOverride` from Firefox 159; before that the preference `layout.css.prefers-color-scheme.content-override` |
| Reduced motion, contrast, forced colours | same command, per page | preferences `ui.prefersReducedMotion`, `browser.display.document_color_use`; relaunch |
| Default font size | `Page.setFontSizes`, per page, experimental | preference `font.size.variable.x-western`; relaunch |
| Minimum font size | launch flag `--blink-settings=minimumFontSize=` | preference `font.minimum-size.x-western`; relaunch |
| Rendered device pixel ratio | `Emulation.setDeviceMetricsOverride` | preference `layout.css.devPixelsPerPx`; the BiDi viewport ratio is reported to the page but not rendered |
| Which rules were exercised | `CSS.startRuleUsageTracking`, `takeCoverageDelta` | none |

Sources for the table: the protocol and source files cited above, plus [Firefox StaticPrefList.yaml](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/modules/libpref/init/StaticPrefList.yaml), [Firefox all.js](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/modules/libpref/init/all.js) and [Chromium settings.json5](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/frame/settings.json5). That the Firefox preferences have the stated effect rests on the local tests described next.

**Local tests, not published findings.** The tests for this report drove headless Chrome 157 and Firefox 160 through onionskin's own drivers against a small test page. These runs are single measurements on one machine and one page, and the scripts were temporary. In Chrome, forced `hover` was visible to in-page `matches()` and `getComputedStyle`, the parent did not match `:hover`, forced `focus` alone did not give `:focus-visible`, a made-up state name was accepted without error, and all tested media features took effect without a reload. `Page.setFontSizes` at 20 px widened a button from 54.5 to 66.1 px and left text sized in px alone. `Emulation.setEmulatedOSTextScale` had no visible effect on a desktop page. In Firefox, the BiDi colour-scheme override worked and reset at once, `prefers-reduced-motion` and `forced-colors` were refused as unsupported, and each preference in the table had its effect after a relaunch. Real input gave the same pseudo-class results in both engines step for step: hover on the element and its ancestors, `:active` while the button is held, mouse focus without `:focus-visible`, Tab focus with it. Two engine differences matter for later sections. With a minimum font size of 12 px, text authored at 6 px was laid out larger in both engines, but `getComputedStyle` reported 12 px in Chrome and still 6 px in Firefox. And with a device pixel ratio of 2 set through BiDi, Firefox reported the ratio to the page but kept the 1x box heights, so onionskin's `:scale` option means different things in the two engines today. Chromium and Edge speak the same protocol as Chrome but were not tested.

### What tools do without the engine's help

No script API forces a pseudo-class. The Storybook pseudo-states addon rewrites style sheet rules so that a class stands in for the pseudo-class, handles eight pseudo-classes, and states its limits: it does not render browser default styles, it stops after 1,000 rewritten selectors per sheet, and it skips cross-origin sheets ([Storybook pseudo-states addon](https://github.com/storybookjs/storybook/tree/next/code/addons/pseudo-states); [rewriteStyleSheet.ts](https://github.com/storybookjs/storybook/blob/next/code/addons/pseudo-states/src/preview/rewriteStyleSheet.ts)). For a tool that tests the page's CSS, rewriting edits the thing under test. Events dispatched by script do not help either: Cypress documents that `.trigger('mouseover')` has no effect in CSS, and Chromatic gives the same reason, that CSS hover needs a trusted event ([Cypress, hover](https://docs.cypress.io/api/commands/hover); [Chromatic, Hover and focus states](https://www.chromatic.com/docs/hoverfocus/)). Script `focus()` works, but Selectors Level 4 leaves `:focus-visible` to browser heuristics that depend on the input that came before, so the result is reproducible only if the tool controls the input history ([Selectors Level 4, :focus-visible](https://drafts.csswg.org/selectors-4/#the-focus-visible-pseudo)). `:active` cannot be reached from script at all.

Mainstream tools therefore use real input for element states and per-page media emulation for preferences. Playwright's `page.emulateMedia` takes media type, colour scheme, reduced motion, forced colours and contrast, with null meaning "leave alone", and has no forced pseudo-class API ([Playwright, page.emulateMedia](https://playwright.dev/docs/api/class-page#page-emulate-media)). BackstopJS adds a declared wait after an interaction, a selector or a time, because hover and click often start transitions ([BackstopJS README](https://github.com/garris/BackstopJS)). Percy stores computed CSS for listed elements but leaves creating the state to the test, which gives no guarantee that the state was active ([Percy, configure pseudo-class](https://www.browserstack.com/docs/percy/percy-snapshot-config/configure-pseudo-class)). **StyleProof is the only tool found that forces pseudo-classes through CDP.** It forces one interactive control at a time, records only the difference from the resting capture, re-reads beyond the control because siblings and `:has()` ancestors can change, bounds the work (800 controls by default), and saves the capture with `statesSkipped: true` when a bound is hit or the browser is Firefox or WebKit ([StyleProof forced-state capture](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof/blob/main/docs/forced-state-capture.md)). Its design is documented but has no independent evaluation. Two traps are documented. Chromatic crops snapshots to the story root, which cuts off focus rings ([Chromatic, Hover and focus states](https://www.chromatic.com/docs/hoverfocus/)); outlines and shadows are painted outside the border box and move no other box, so a focus ring shows in a style diff and not in a box diff. And Chromatic ties a baseline to a mode's name, not its values, so changing a mode's viewport keeps comparing against the old baseline ([Chromatic, Modes](https://www.chromatic.com/docs/modes/)).

### Font size is its own parameter, and zoom belongs with width

WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.4 requires that text can be resized to 200% without loss of content or function, and lists clipped or truncated content at 200% as a failure ([W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/resize-text.html)). The Web Almanac reports px as 67% of font-size units on desktop sites (the figure was read from a summary of the chapter, and its exact basis was not checked), which suggests fixed heights and containers that a larger default size will break ([Web Almanac 2025](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/accessibility)). No peer-reviewed study of text-resize failures on web pages was found. The direct evidence is from Android, where dVermin compares each view at the default and at a larger display scale and reports **97% precision and 97% recall** for pages with scaling issues ([Su et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3551349.3556935)), and from VizAssert, which varied the default font size from 16 to 32 px ([Panchekha et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3192366.3192407)). The dVermin oracle fits onionskin as it stands: capture at the default and at a larger font size, then require that chosen relations still hold. Page zoom is different. For layout, 200% zoom equals half the viewport width at twice the device pixel ratio, so it belongs with the width work in the next section.

### Coverage turns "what was captured" into a number

Chromium records a rule as used when it matched an element during style resolution, and `CSS.takeCoverageDelta` returns the rules that became used since the last call ([Chromium inspector_css_agent.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/inspector/inspector_css_agent.cc)). In the local test, the delta at rest listed the base rules, and after `button:hover` was forced the next delta held exactly that rule. One caution from the same test: the `a:visited` rule was reported as used although no link was visited. StyleProof does the equivalent bookkeeping per rule and can fail a run when classes defined in the CSS were never rendered ([StyleProof REFERENCE.md](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof/blob/main/docs/REFERENCE.md)). BiDi has no coverage command, so Firefox would need a weaker page-script pass in CILLA's style that tests whether a selector could match.

**What onionskin should take.** Identify every capture by a map of independent parameters (page, width, height, engine, preferences, default font size, state) and let that map be the key, with names only as labels. Use CSS's own words: pseudo-class names for states, media feature names and values for preferences. Offer forced states in Chrome for speed, and real input in both engines as the common base, with a declared settle condition after the input. Name mouse focus (`focus`) and keyboard focus (`focus` plus `focus-visible`) as separate states. Capture a state as a delta against the resting capture, bound the work, and record in the capture every requested parameter the engine could not honour. Read font-size effects from boxes, not from computed `font-size`. Report never-exercised rules from Chrome's coverage data. Do not multiply axes by default: the resting capture plus single-axis variants (dark, larger font, each state), with the full product opt-in. **What to avoid:** rewriting the style sheets under test, script-dispatched events as a stand-in for hover, baselines keyed by a user-chosen name alone, silently capturing the resting state when a forced state failed, paint-level emulation commands for style capture, and treating forced `hover` on one node as equal to a real pointer.

## Six fixed widths missed 9 of 48 seeded regressions that range facts caught

The second half of the first direction is to state layout facts over ranges of viewport widths, as ReDeCheck does. The published numbers on what a short list of widths loses are the strongest quantitative evidence in this report. In the journal study of the method, a diff of range-tagged graphs found all 48 seeded regressions on 15 pages with one false positive, while the same diff at six fixed widths (480, 600, 640, 768, 1024 and 1280 px) **missed 9 of the 48**, and manual checks at those widths missed 6. Among 36 changes visible at 10 widths or fewer, the range diff missed 4 and the six-width diff missed 20 ([Walsh et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1748), Tables III and IV). On 26 live pages, the preset widths of five responsive testing tools showed between 66% and 81% of the 33 distinct failures, so **19% to 34% would be missed**, and five failures were visible at none of 42 tried widths ([Walsh et al. 2017a](https://doi.org/10.1145/3092703.3092712), Table 2). onionskin captures one width per capture today, so these numbers describe its current blind spot.

### The model and its one weak assumption

The responsive layout graph has one node per element and one edge per parent-child or sibling pair. A node carries visibility constraints (x1, x2), inclusive ranges of widths where the element is shown. An edge carries alignment constraints (x1, x2, type, attribute set), and two constraints of one edge may not overlap, so at any width at most one holds ([Walsh et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1748), section 3.1). The first paper had a third kind that later papers dropped: a width constraint that fits a line, element width = m% of the container's width + c, over a range ([Walsh et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/ASE.2015.31)). It is the only published attempt to give a quantity a range, and it suits fluid layouts. Ranges are built by walking the sampled widths in ascending order, comparing each sample's graph with the previous one, and binary-searching between the two widths wherever a node or an attribute set differs. The released code caches every captured width so that all searches share captures, and it resizes one live page without reloading it ([ReDeCheck ResponsiveLayoutGraph.java](https://github.com/redecheck/redecheck/blob/d3f2364960aa7398f7085a502576da70759f5ed0/src/main/java/shef/rlg/ResponsiveLayoutGraph.java); [ReDeCheck Tool.java](https://github.com/redecheck/redecheck/blob/d3f2364960aa7398f7085a502576da70759f5ed0/src/main/java/shef/main/Tool.java)). The diff between two versions classes each mismatch as a bounds difference (same relations, different range), an attributes difference or both, and reports the symmetric difference of the two ranges as the widths to look at.

The method assumes each fact changes at most once between two neighbouring samples. The paper admits that it "could potentially fail to sample certain layout behaviours that occur between two consecutive sample widths". A reading of the code for this report adds a second case, which is an inference and not a published finding: when a fact goes through three values between two samples, the search finds where the first value ends and labels the rest with the last value, so the middle range is lost. Re-checking each found boundary against the upper sample, and searching again when they differ, closes most of that gap.

### What sampling costs, and what breakpoints do not tell

ReDeCheck's defaults are a 60 px grid from 320 to 1400 px, plus the widths on both sides of every `min-width` and `max-width` breakpoint, plus binary search ([ReDeCheck CommandLineParser.java](https://github.com/redecheck/redecheck/blob/d3f2364960aa7398f7085a502576da70759f5ed0/src/main/java/shef/main/CommandLineParser.java)). The journal study compared nine step sizes on 450 mutants. Capturing all 1,001 widths took 89 to 148 s per page (mean 108 s), while grid sampling with search averaged under 30 s at every step size and for several pages used under 100 widths. Detection hardly depended on the step, and the authors recommend 60 or 80 px as a global step ([Walsh et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1748), RQ3). That optimum reflects one browser resizing in place in 2015 to 2017; it is a cost trade-off from 15 pages, not a law. Adding breakpoint widths improved detection on six pages but saved few searches, because "many layout changes observed during the extraction of the RLG could be at viewport widths not programmed as breakpoints in the site's CSS files". The later tools from the same line gave up sampling: LocaliCSS states that it captures every width from 320 to 1400 px, and says it copies Layout Dr's detection in this, a claim that was not found in the Layout Dr paper itself ([Zerin et al. 2025a](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25476); [Althomali et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICST53961.2022.00025)).

ReDeCheck's own breakpoint extraction is fragile. It downloads only linked style sheets, parses them outside the browser, accepts only px and em (em times 16), and silently skips what it does not understand ([ReDeCheck RLGExtractor.java](https://github.com/redecheck/redecheck/blob/d3f2364960aa7398f7085a502576da70759f5ed0/src/main/java/shef/main/RLGExtractor.java)). Chrome offers better: `CSS.getMediaQueries`, which carries no experimental mark of its own although the CSS domain as a whole does, returns every media query the engine holds with its source (media rule, import rule, linked or inline sheet), whether it is active, and each length as a computed pixel value ([CDP CSS domain](https://github.com/ChromeDevTools/devtools-protocol/blob/master/pdl/domains/CSS.pdl)). The returned expression has a feature name and a value but no comparison operator, so for range syntax such as `(width >= 600px)` the direction must come from the query text; how Chrome fills the expression for range syntax was not confirmed. Sampling the nearest integers below, at and above each computed value avoids the question. Three specification facts shape the design. Relative units in media queries follow the browser's default font size, not the page's styles, so em breakpoints move when the default font size is varied ([Media Queries Level 4, Units](https://www.w3.org/TR/mediaqueries-4/#units)). `min-` and `max-` bounds are inclusive, and the specification warns that pairing `max-width: 320px` with `min-width: 321px` leaves fractional widths where neither applies, which is why Bootstrap writes `767.98px` ([Media Queries Level 4](https://www.w3.org/TR/mediaqueries-4/#mq-min-max); [Bootstrap breakpoints](https://getbootstrap.com/docs/5.3/layout/breakpoints/)). And breakpoints applied by JavaScript are in no style sheet: Applitools and Percy both have options to re-capture the DOM at listed widths for that reason ([Applitools runner configuration](https://applitools.com/tutorials/playwright/api/runner-configuration); [Percy responsive DOM](https://www.browserstack.com/docs/percy/advanced-snapshots/responsive-dom)). It follows that loading a page fresh at each width, as onionskin does, and resizing a live page, as ReDeCheck does, can give different layouts, and a range fact should say which was done.

CSS has also moved further from the assumption that layout changes at media query breakpoints. Container queries switch on a container's size and have been widely available since February 2023 ([MDN @container](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@container)), `clamp()` with viewport units changes sizes at every width ([MDN clamp()](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/clamp)), and grid `auto-fit` changes column counts at widths that appear in no style sheet ([MDN repeat()](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/repeat)). **No peer-reviewed work and no dedicated tool was found for testing container queries or for locating change points other than sample-and-search**; the 2025 follow-up papers work only with media-query ranges ([Zerin et al. 2025a](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25476); [Zerin et al. 2025b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00678)). CDP exposes the container queries on a rule and `DOM.getContainerForNode`, all experimental and untested here, and has no command that lists every container query. The practical consequence is that sample-and-search stays the base method, and extracted breakpoints serve as extra sample points and as labels for change points: declared (media query), derived (container query) or emergent (wrap, column count, `clamp()` knee).

Every tool in the ReDeCheck line varies width only and fixes the height: 600 px in ReDeCheck's code, 1,000 px in VERVE and Layout Dr ([Althomali et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1756); [Althomali et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICST53961.2022.00025)). Only VizAssert treats height and default font size as ranges, by proof ([Panchekha et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3192366.3192407)). Height still matters to a width range: the `orientation` media feature is portrait when height is at least the width, so a fixed capture height creates a hidden breakpoint at that width ([Media Queries Level 4](https://www.w3.org/TR/mediaqueries-4/)). Android's guidance classifies width and height separately and calls width the more important ([Android, window size classes](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/layouts/adaptive/use-window-size-classes)). Nothing found treats device pixel ratio as a range.

### Reports by range, and the small-range detector's poor record

ReDeCheck prints one line per element pair with XPaths and a width range, and takes a screenshot at the middle of the range ([ReDeCheck README](https://github.com/redecheck/redecheck)). Its own data show the format's fault: 328 reports on 26 pages came down to 33 distinct failures over 137 distinct ranges, and **one icon grid produced 147 reports of a single failure** ([Walsh et al. 2017a](https://doi.org/10.1145/3092703.3092712)). The authors count reading cost in distinct ranges, 4.2 per real failure, which is their own count and not an observation of developers; no user study of range reports exists. The follow-up tools moved the inspection width from the midpoint to the narrowest width of the range, on the grounds that failures show more where space is tight, and Layout Dr works from the two bordering widths just outside a failure range ([Althomali et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1756); [Althomali et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICST53961.2022.00025)). In Layout Dr's study, 17 of 55 failure ranges started at 320 px, the bottom of the tested interval, so the end of a tested interval means "unknown beyond here", not a boundary.

The small-range detector flags a relation that holds for at most five or six consecutive widths with a different relation on each side. Across 45 pages it raised 248 reports, 152 true and 96 false; 147 of the true ones were the one icon grid, **only 4 of 45 pages had a real small-range failure**, and on 20 further pages all 53 reports were false ([Althomali et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1756)). The authors blame chance alignment within tolerance. The main cause they name for true cases, two media queries that are both active at one width, can be found from the extracted queries without rendering. Small-range layouts belong in onionskin as an opt-in hint computed from coarse position relations, not as a default check.

The model has no maintained implementation. ReDeCheck's last commit is from 24 November 2018, the group's work ended in 2022 with automated repair, and nobody outside it has extended the model. One group has since built CSS fault localisation and LLM repair on the same failure ranges (LocaliCSS at ENASE 2025; ReDeFix in the ICSME 2025 new-ideas track; both read here as arXiv versions), and ReFLAIR (FSE 2026) uses ReDeCheck as a baseline for a two-width reflow check ([He et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3808136)). No independent replication, industrial report or outside critique was found, though the search had no access to citation indexes. A 2020 paper that writes ReDeCheck's failure types as Cornipickle rules over resized snapshots is known here only from the authors' slides and is unconfirmed ([Beroual et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-50578-3_1)).

No tool has a syntax for "this relation holds from width a to width b". Galen tags spec sections with names such as `mobile` and binds each name to one browser size in the test suite, so "mobile" is checked at one width ([Galen spec language guide](http://galenframework.com/docs/reference-galen-spec-language-guide/); [Galen test suite syntax](http://galenframework.com/docs/reference-galen-test-suite-syntax/)). Visual tools take lists of widths. The range forms in use are CSS's own and Bootstrap's named breakpoints with `up`, `only` and `between`. onionskin's specs are Clojure data and already write distances as `[low high]`, so a width option of the same shape, with ranges named once in a map, needs no parser.

**What onionskin should take.** Store each fact family (x relation, y relation, containment, each alignment) per element pair as a sorted list of runs `[from to value]` with inclusive integer bounds, which gives the non-overlap rule for free and makes a diff a merge of two run lists. Sample a grid plus both sides of every extracted breakpoint in parallel, refine where neighbouring samples differ, re-check each boundary, cache by width, and offer an exhaustive mode for release checks. Extract breakpoints in the browser, per capture configuration, and report queries that were not understood. State the height, scale, default font size, engine and load-or-resize mode with every range. Report by range and change point, name the narrowest and the two bordering widths, and say what was sampled. Let expectations carry a width range and add both ends of each stated range to the sample set. **What to avoid:** the single-change assumption, the attribute set as part of a key, equating "breakpoint" with "media query", one report line per pair, a fixed "small" threshold presented as a fact, and width tags bound to one size.

## Three in four reported box overlaps were invisible to a person

The second direction is checks that need no spec. The research gives exact rules for each check and, for a few of them, measured accuracy. The measurements say the same thing from several sides: a rule over element boxes alone is mostly wrong about what a person sees, and the checks that hold up are those that look at text and at the viewport.

### ReDeCheck's five rules and their record

ReDeCheck never judges one rendering alone. Each rule compares a layout with the same page at the next wider viewport, and the authors state the intent test in one sentence: "If the elements always overlap, the effect is likely intended and/or easily noticed by a developer in a manual spotcheck. If the elements overlap infrequently, however, a subtle RLF is likely to be occurring" ([Walsh et al. 2017a](https://doi.org/10.1145/3092703.3092712)). A collision is a sibling pair that overlaps over a range and does not overlap one pixel wider. An element protrusion is a pair that overlaps as siblings where one was the other's ancestor at the wider width. A viewport protrusion is an element that is visible at widths where it has no containing parent, meaning it lies outside `body`. A wrapping failure is a child that sits in its parent's row at the wider range and outside it at the adjacent narrower range while a row of at least two remains. The released code adds conditions the paper leaves out, such as skipping elements that are outside the viewport at every width, and it contains a filter for overlaps of exactly 1 px whose call is commented out ([ReDeCheck RLGAnalyser.java](https://github.com/redecheck/redecheck/blob/d3f2364960aa7398f7085a502576da70759f5ed0/src/main/java/shef/analysis/RLGAnalyser.java)).

On 26 live pages the tool produced 328 reports: 197 true positives, 48 false positives and 83 "non-observable issues", which are true of the boxes and invisible to a person. A manual classification of its reports on 20 further pages followed in 2021. The table combines both.

| Type | 26 pages: true / false / non-observable | 20 further pages: true / false / non-observable | Share a person can see (computed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Element collision | 8 / 0 / 24 | 2 / 0 / 10 | 9 of 43, 21% |
| Element protrusion | 3 / 0 / 36 | 0 / 0 / 51 | 3 of 90, 3% |
| Viewport protrusion | 24 / 0 / 23 | 8 / 0 / 2 | 32 of 57, 56% |
| Wrapping | 10 / 5 / 0 | 4 / 11 / 2 | 14 of 31, 45% |
| Small-range | 152 / 43 / 0 | 0 / 53 / 0 | 152 of 248, 61%; 5 of 96 without one icon grid |

Sources: [Walsh et al. 2017a](https://doi.org/10.1145/3092703.3092712), Table 1b, and [Althomali et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1756), Table III. The last column was computed for this report from those tables over 45 pages (one page of the first set is excluded, as in the 2021 paper); it is not a published figure. The paper reports no recall: "Our appraisal of the prototype tool does not include the possibility of false negatives".

The three overlap-based types had no false positives at the level of boxes, and most of their reports still did not matter. The causes the authors name are transparent backgrounds around content that does not overlap, borders or padding in the page's background colour, `overflow: hidden` on the container, and empty elements. No paper counts how often each cause occurred, and none measures z-index, `pointer-events` or `clip-path` separately. The two alignment-based types, small-range and wrapping, carried all the false positives. A 2026 measurement on a different task shows how little the reports say about lost content: run at 320 px only and scored against hand-labelled reflow problems, ReDeCheck had 7.29% precision and 4.23% recall ([He et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3808136), figures as read from an extracted table).

Viser and Verve check each report against pixels. They set the involved elements to `opacity: 0` one at a time, which keeps the layout unchanged, screenshot the area of concern, and call the report visible only if the layers differ ([Althomali et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICST.2019.00027)). Of 117 collision and protrusion reports on 20 pages, **83 (71%) were non-observable** by manual classification, and on 20 further pages 63 of 73 were. Viser agreed with the human labels in 87.2% of cases at the narrowest width of the range, 82.9% at the middle and 77.8% at the widest; Verve reached 86.3% on the first pages and 91.8% on the new ones for the three overlap types ([Althomali et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1756)). Seven of Viser's 15 disagreements were differences of a few pixels that people had waved through, and the authors found that whether "n pixels of overlap are negligible" stays "to a great extent, subjective". One Viser miss is instructive: a container with `overflow: hidden` cut off protruding content, no pixel changed where Viser looked, and a person still counted it as a failure because content was lost. Both tools are unmaintained (last pushes April 2019 and September 2022).

### Rules from verification, text scaling and platform tools

VizAssert states its spec-free properties as one-line formulas: no box's right edge beyond the root's right edge (no horizontal scrolling), no two non-empty text boxes overlapping, and interactive boxes on screen, where "on screen" is one-sided because a box below or to the right can be scrolled to and a box above or to the left cannot ([Panchekha et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3192366.3192407); [VizAssert assertions file](https://github.com/uwplse/Cassius/blob/master/bench/assertions/assertions.vizassert)). On 62 template pages at widths from 1,024 px, horizontal scrolling was verified absent on 60. The text-overlap rule verified 53 pages and gave **2 true violations against 5 false positives**, which the authors explain: text boxes reserve space for descenders that the glyphs do not use. On 38 of the 62 pages, text for screen readers was placed off screen on purpose. Cassius, the artifact, is a research prototype last pushed in February 2023.

Work on text expansion compares the page with itself under a changed setting, so it needs no stored baseline. AccessiText runs each Android screen at the default and at a scaled text size and applies five rules; the overlap rule reports only overlap that is absent at the default, and ignores floating action buttons. On 30 apps its precision was 89.34% for overlapping views (122 reports), 73.13% for cropped views (67) and 100% for truncated views (11), with OCR errors named as the main cause of false cropped reports ([Alshayban and Malek 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3540250.3549118)). Android Studio's locale analyzer reports text that is "ellipsized in locale X but not in default locale" and text whose lines need more room than the view has, again only when the base rendering did not have the problem ([Android Studio LocaleAnalyzer.kt](https://github.com/JetBrains/android/blob/master/visual-lint/src/com/android/tools/visuallint/analyzers/LocaleAnalyzer.kt)). GWALI, covered in the earlier research, does the same between a page and its translation ([Alameer et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICST.2016.36)).

Rule-based checks built into platforms are simple, and each ships with an intent filter. Android Studio's overlap analyzer considers only a text view covered by a sibling that is drawn above it, and reports when at least half the text bounds are covered; its bounds analyzer flags a child outside its parent but skips scrolling views and everything below them ([OverlapAnalyzer.kt](https://github.com/JetBrains/android/blob/master/visual-lint/src/com/android/tools/visuallint/analyzers/OverlapAnalyzer.kt); [BoundsAnalyzer.kt](https://github.com/JetBrains/android/blob/master/visual-lint/src/com/android/tools/visuallint/analyzers/BoundsAnalyzer.kt)). LayoutTest-iOS tests sibling subviews only, with an epsilon of 1e-5, exempts the subviews of switches, text views and buttons, and prints the remedy in the failure message: "If this is intentional, you should add one of the views to viewsAllowingOverlap" ([LayoutTest-iOS LYTLayoutTestCase.m](https://github.com/linkedin/LayoutTest-iOS/blob/master/LayoutTest/TestCase/LYTLayoutTestCase.m)). Flutter reports overflow of a flex container along its main axis ([Flutter flex.dart](https://github.com/flutter/flutter/blob/master/packages/flutter/lib/src/rendering/flex.dart)). **None of these platform rules has a published precision.** Learned detectors on screenshots report about 0.85 precision and recall on their own data, with text overlap the weakest class, and OwlEyes' pilot study of 10,330 crowd-testing screenshots found display issues in 4,470, of which 47% were component occlusion and 21% text overlap ([Liu et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01417)).

### Clipped text needs fractional text boxes

Three signals exist for clipped or truncated content. The cheapest, `scrollWidth > clientWidth`, uses integers, and a CSS Working Group issue records that rounding can make the two equal while an ellipsis shows; whether that issue has since been resolved was not checked ([CSSWG issue 4123, list archive](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-css-archive/2019Jul/0300.html); [MDN scrollWidth](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Element/scrollWidth)). Ant Design avoids scroll sizes: it appends an empty probe element and reports truncation when the probe's fractional rectangle lies outside the element's ([Ant Design typography util.ts](https://github.com/ant-design/ant-design/blob/master/components/typography/Base/util.ts)). The precise signal is the text's own boxes from a `Range`, compared with the padding box of the nearest ancestor that clips. Style facts then set the meaning: under CSS Overflow Level 3, `overflow: hidden` clips but stays scrollable by script, `clip` forbids scrolling, and `text-overflow: ellipsis` affects rendering only ([CSS Overflow Module Level 3](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-overflow-3/)). No source measures the precision of any DOM-based clipped-text rule on web pages.

Browser tools mostly show overflow instead of judging it. Firefox has badged the elements that cause a scroll container to overflow since version 83 ([Firefox source docs](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/devtools-user/page_inspector/how_to/debug_scrollable_overflow/index.html)), and Chrome badges scroll containers only ([Chrome DevTools badges](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/elements/badges)). **Lighthouse removed both of its layout-like audits in version 12.0.0**: `content-width`, which its own documentation called an indirect check, went with the PWA category, and `tap-targets` was replaced by axe's `target-size` as "no longer a priority" ([Lighthouse 12.0.0 release notes](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/releases/tag/v12.0.0)). The `tap-targets` gatherer is still worth reading for the filters real use forced on it: drop targets nested in other targets, drop links inside text blocks ("cause a lot of failures"), neutralise fixed and sticky elements, then hit-test the centre of each rectangle ([Lighthouse tap-targets gatherer, v11.7.1](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/v11.7.1/core/gather/gatherers/seo/tap-targets.js)).

The known sources of false alarms are well documented. Screen-reader-only text is positioned off screen or clipped to one pixel on purpose ([WebAIM, invisible content](https://webaim.org/techniques/css/invisiblecontent/)). WCAG's reflow criterion exempts content that needs two dimensions, such as maps, data tables and toolbars, and accepts truncation when a link leads to the full text ([W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.10](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html)). Scroll containers state that content may exceed the box. Off-canvas panels are meant to be outside, yet two practitioner articles name off-screen menus without a clipping ancestor as a real cause of page overflow ([Coyier 2014](https://css-tricks.com/findingfixing-unintended-body-overflow/); [Polypane blog](https://polypane.app/blog/strategies-for-dealing-with-horizontal-overflows/)). Fixed and sticky elements cut both ways: Lighthouse removes them before testing, and WCAG's guidance shows a fixed advert covering a focused link as a defect.

### Only three of thirteen intent filters have numbers

Every tool that reports overlap uses at least one structural filter. Thirteen were found in use: report only overlap that is absent at the adjacent wider width, or absent at the default setting; compare siblings only; never count an ancestor and its descendant; require the offender to be painted later; hit-test the victim; accept only text as a victim, covered by a minimum share; skip targets with the same action; exempt composite controls; remove fixed and sticky elements first; require a minimum depth; look for a pixel change under an opacity toggle; and keep an allow list. **Only the two cross-setting filters and the opacity toggle were measured.** ReDeCheck's cross-width filter gave no box-level false positives and still left 24 of 32 collisions invisible. AccessiText's default-against-scaled filter reached 89.34% precision. The opacity toggle agreed with people in 86% to 92% of cases. No controlled comparison of the filters exists, and no source supports z-index or `position` values as signals of intent. axe-core shows how a mature tool handles what it cannot decide: besides passes and violations it returns "incomplete" results that need review, for example when an element is partly obscured or there are too many overlapping elements, and it grades violations by impact ([axe-core API](https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core/blob/develop/doc/API.md); [axe-core target-size check](https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core/blob/develop/lib/checks/mobile/target-size.json)).

Wrapping is the least reliable rule: 10 true and 5 false reports on the first pages, then 4 true, 11 false and 2 non-observable on the next twenty. People rejected footer links that are meant to wrap, elements that never formed a row, and icons that wrap pleasingly. A stricter filter (a row of at least three, the wrapped element below all of them, all visible) raised agreement with people from 35.3% to 64.7%, and the authors concede that "it is difficult to judge the developer's intent" ([Althomali et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1756)). No source tests CSS signals such as `flex-wrap`. Where the author declared that items must not wrap, the failure shows up as overflow, which the other checks catch.

Models do not rescue the judgement. A plainly prompted GPT-4.1 scored 6.67% precision and 0.47% recall on web reflow problems, typically declaring the page fine; ReFLAIR reached 80.49% precision and 95.31% recall on 24 pages by comparing DOM-derived widgets across two renderings and exploring menus, at an average cost of about 1.18 US dollars per page ([He et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3808136)). A CVPR 2026 benchmark of ten general models on app screens reports F1 of 20.36% for text overflow and 31.21% for container overlap; only its poster page was read ([Xiang et al. 2026](https://cvpr.thecvf.com/virtual/2026/poster/38861)). The geometric judgements onionskin can make exactly are the ones these models are worst at.

**What onionskin should take.** A short default set, ordered by evidence: the page scrolls sideways at a captured width, with the culprit elements named; text cut through by a clipping ancestor that has no ellipsis or clamp; and text overlapping text by more than the depth of a descender. Then, as warnings, text or a control covered by a non-ancestor element confirmed by a hit test. Use text boxes and replaced content as the possible victims, never bare container boxes. Use the cross-width and cross-setting comparison as the intent filter: a finding that holds at every captured width, or already in the unstressed capture, is taken as designed. Return three verdicts (fail, review, information) with the measured amount, the width range and a reason, group findings by common ancestor and range, ship a default exemption list as data (screen-reader-only pattern, scroll containers, fixed and sticky, the unreachable side), and print the allow-list form in the failure message. **What to avoid:** child-outside-parent as a failure (3% visible), wrapping and small-range checks in the default set, `scrollWidth` for text truncation, any-pixel-differs confirmation, intent inferred from z-index or `position`, containment inferred from geometry, OCR, and a model in the default loop.

## Absolute paths lost 67% to 79% of elements; subtree matching survives a wrapper

The third direction starts from a weakness in what onionskin ships. A capture records a path, a box, styles and pseudo-elements for each node, and the diff pairs nodes by exact path. One added wrapper `div` changes the path of every element below it, so the diff reports them all as removed and added. Locator research has measured how badly structural paths age. Across one release step of six applications, **absolute XPaths broke for 67% of 675 target elements**, against 12% for the ROBULA+ algorithm ([Leotta et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICST.2015.7102611)). On 598 elements from the home pages of 40 popular sites, with versions 12 to 60 months apart, absolute XPaths failed for **79%**, and a weighted comparison of 14 element properties (Similo) failed for 12% ([Nass et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3571855)). Those benchmarks span far more change than one pull request, so the absolute figures are pessimistic for onionskin by an unknown amount, but the ranking is the same in every study: the absolute path is the weakest signal measured. StyleProof recorded the failure in its own history. In its version 6.2.2 a wrapper "re-keyed the button's structural path", the button appeared as removed and added, and a real colour change on it "vanished" from the certified diff ([StyleProof re-nesting proof](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof/blob/main/docs/proof/re-nesting/README.md)).

### Which algorithms survive a wrapper or a move

Exact tree edit distance is the textbook answer and the wrong tool. RTED and its successor APTED need cubic time and quadratic memory, and their edit model has insert, delete and rename but no move ([APTED project page](http://tree-edit-distance.dbresearch.uni-salzburg.at/)). In GumTree's evaluation on source files, RTED gave no result within memory and time limits for about 17% of cases ([Falleri et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2642937.2642982)). On home pages of the Alexa top 1,000 sites, APTED hit a 450-second timeout on large pages and ran up to 100 times slower than the heuristic it was compared with ([Brisset et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.is.2022.102126), read as [arXiv 2004.12821](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12821)). An added wrapper is cheap in the edit model, one insert, but a subtree that moves to another parent is deleted and re-inserted. GumTree keeps an exact algorithm only as a bounded fallback, on subtrees under 20 nodes in its hybrid matcher ([GumTree HybridBottomUpMatcher.java](https://github.com/GumTreeDiff/gumtree/blob/main/core/src/main/java/com/github/gumtreediff/matchers/heuristic/gt/HybridBottomUpMatcher.java)).

GumTree's two phases fit the problem. The top-down phase pairs identical subtrees, tallest first, using a subtree hash followed by an exact comparison; a pair with no rival is mapped at once with all its descendants. The bottom-up phase then pairs containers: an unmapped node is paired with the unmapped node of the same label that shares the largest share of mapped descendants, if that share (the dice value) passes a threshold. The paper used a minimum height of 2 and a minimum dice of 0.5 ([Falleri et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2642937.2642982)). This is why a wrapper does no harm: the subtrees below it are identical and pair wherever they sit, the old parent finds the new parent through them, and the wrapper stays unpaired. The 2024 "simple" variant, now GumTree's default, replaces the exact recovery step with three cheap rules for the children of a new pair: align identical children by longest common subsequence, then children of the same shape, then children whose type is unique on both sides. It also finds candidates by walking up from the partners of mapped descendants, so nothing compares all pairs ([Falleri and Martinez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3597503.3639148); [GumTree SimpleBottomUpMatcher.java](https://github.com/GumTreeDiff/gumtree/blob/main/core/src/main/java/com/github/gumtreediff/matchers/heuristic/gt/SimpleBottomUpMatcher.java)). The paper's full text was not read; the conference listing and the authors' slides disagree on the size of the speedup. GumTree is maintained (v4.1.0, 9 October 2026).

Two cautions apply. An independent study found inaccurate mappings in 20% to 29% of 263,165 Java file revisions for GumTree, mostly where many subtrees look alike ([Fan et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00141)), and HTML has more look-alike subtrees than code: list items, table rows, icon buttons. And **no peer-reviewed measurement of any of these algorithms on HTML or DOM trees was found.** The one tool that applies GumTree to captured DOM trees is sosie, whose design document says its first phase "handles 80-95% of nodes in a typical refactoring" and estimates 50 to 500 ms at 5,000 nodes; both are claims without a dataset ([sosie design document](https://github.com/cuihtlauac/sosie/blob/main/sosie-design.md)). Its code offers practices worth copying: it keeps only pairs found in both directions, removes pairs that break ancestor order, tests four invariants on all tree pairs up to five nodes, and reports wrappers and moves as informational entries ([sosie gumtree.ml](https://github.com/cuihtlauac/sosie/blob/main/lib/gumtree.ml)).

Other families fail in known ways. React's reconciliation and morphdom compare the children of already matched parents, so a wrapper at a child's position makes them rebuild or mispair the subtree ([React, Reconciliation](https://legacy.reactjs.org/docs/reconciliation.html); [morphdom](https://github.com/patrick-steele-idem/morphdom)). XyDiff, like GumTree, starts from signatures of identical subtrees, heaviest first, in O(n log n) time, and adds a rule worth keeping: a node with an id may only match a node with the same id ([Cobéna et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICDE.2002.994696)). idiomorph identifies a parent without an id by the ids of its descendants, the keyed counterpart of GumTree's container step ([idiomorph README](https://github.com/bigskysoftware/idiomorph)). Flexible tree matching drops the ancestry constraint and scores every pair by label similarity; people agreed with each other on only 78.3% of matches between different page designs ([Kumar et al. 2011](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2011/445)), and the authors of its successor report that the original took an hour on a toy example of 58 nodes ([arXiv 2004.12821](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12821)). Its fast successor SFTM, used for locator repair in Erratum, found the right element in 87.0% of 49,305 synthetic cases and 78.3% of a labelled real sample, against 42.1% and 51.9% for the older WATER; it depends on many attributes per element, publishes no values for its six or more parameters, searches at random, and its errors came almost only from structural change ([Brisset et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.04916), preprint version read).

### Which signals deserve trust

Every multi-property matcher in the testing literature trusts tag, id, name and visible text most and structural paths least. Similo set its weights by hand: 1.5 for tag, id, name, visible text and neighbour text; 0.5 for class, link target, position, size and both kinds of XPath ([Nass et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3571855)). When a replication fitted weights to 10,376 element pairs from 30 applications, visible text, name, type and aria-label came out high, the absolute XPath stayed low (0.05 to 1.05), class was dropped in one configuration, and id varied widely (0.5 to 2.85). Where present, `type` was unchanged in 96% of pairs and `aria-label` in 82% ([Kluge and Stocco 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16424), arXiv version read; the authors give Empirical Software Engineering as the venue). Class being a poor signal suits onionskin, since CSS refactors rename classes and those are the changes it is meant to check. No study reports how often id, class, name, text and tag each change between versions, and none measures how long test ids survive; Playwright's statement that test ids are "the most resilient way of testing" is a documentation claim ([Playwright locators](https://playwright.dev/docs/locators)).

On keys the tools agree in mechanism. GumTree's id matcher pairs two nodes only when the id occurs exactly once in each tree ([GumTree IdMatcher.java](https://github.com/GumTreeDiff/gumtree/blob/main/core/src/main/java/com/github/gumtreediff/matchers/heuristic/IdMatcher.java)). StyleProof hashes `id`, `data-testid` and the first token of a `data-style` attribute into an element's identity, and an element whose identity was replaced stays unpaired and fails closed ([StyleProof REFERENCE.md](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof/blob/main/docs/REFERENCE.md)).

Rendered properties can rescue a pairing, with one risk: a signal that is also the thing being diffed pulls the matcher towards pairs that did not change. StyleProof uses text and geometry only as late steps among elements left unpaired, and only when the signature is unique on both sides: first tag plus own text, then tag, box and own-text length, with the text kept out of the findings ([StyleProof CHANGELOG](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). sosie uses no rendered property at all. Similarity matchers report the downside of position: a new element placed where the old one was can win over the true element that moved ([Kluge and Stocco 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16424)). A wrapper without padding has the same box as its only child, which VON Similo's rule for "same thing on screen" (intersection over union above 0.85) captures, and which means a geometry rule must also require the same tag ([Nass et al. 2023b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.03863)). No surveyed tool matches on computed style, and no study measures how often geometry-based pairing is right after a markup change.

The literature separates three outcomes, a correct pair, a wrong pair and no pair, and treats the wrong pair as the worst because it fails silently. In one benchmark of 733 test breakages, 38 were "mis-selections" where the locator found a different element and the test went on ([Stocco et al. 2018](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/amesbah/resources/papers/fse18-vista.pdf)). Erratum's authors prefer no match to a mismatch because it warns the developer, while Similo always returns its best candidate and its authors call choosing a rejection threshold "non-trivial". Language models have been tried only as a second judge over a cheap matcher's top ten candidates. GPT-4 raised VON Similo from 91.3% to 95.0% of 804 element pairs at about 2 seconds and 4.5 US cents per element ([Nass et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02046)), and a tuned variant without a model later reached 95.51% on the same benchmark ([Kluge and Stocco 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16424)). A deterministic diff has no use for a model call.

**What onionskin should take.** Record a little identity per element: the configured key attributes, `id`, `name`, `type`, `aria-label` and a hash of the element's own text. Then pair in falling order of trust: if the two trees are identical, pair by path as today; pair keys that are unique in both captures; pair identical subtrees, largest first, and repeated ones in document order when their counts agree; pair containers through their paired descendants with GumTree simple's size-dependent threshold; align the remaining children; and only then try a unique tag and text, and a unique tag and box. Attach to every pair the rule that made it, report unpaired elements, wrappers and moves as their own entries, and say that the markup changed too much when the unpaired share is high. Test the matcher without a browser by applying known changes to a captured tree. **What to avoid:** whole-page tree edit distance, lockstep comparison of children as the main method, path equality once the tree shapes differ (the wrapper takes the old element's path), weighted sums that mix geometry with identity, a matcher that always returns its best guess, class names or computed styles as identity, and any model call inside the diff. The speed of such a matcher in Clojure was not measured; milliseconds at 6,000 nodes is an estimate.

## Across engines, relations within 1 px are the claim that holds

The rest of the third direction concerns what a diff reports once elements are paired: which changes it leaves out, how it tells a declared change from one that follows from layout, and what it can say when the two captures come from different browser engines.

### Filters that survive change are tied to elements and listed in the report

Every tool ends up with the same four controls: exclude an element or subtree, lower the strictness for a region, ignore a kind of change, and approve the rest by hand. BackstopJS separates hiding an element, which keeps its place in the layout, from removing it, which does not ([BackstopJS README](https://github.com/garris/BackstopJS/blob/master/README.md)). Chromatic ignores elements by attribute, class or selector list and states the limit: "dimension changes of this element still trigger a change" ([Chromatic, ignoring elements](https://www.chromatic.com/docs/ignoring-elements/)). Percy gives a region one of four rules and warns that a hand-drawn region at fixed coordinates may be wrong at other widths or browsers ([Percy, define regions](https://www.browserstack.com/docs/percy/set-regions/define-regions)). Applitools applies match levels per region, among them Layout, which checks only relative positions, and a level where text must match a pattern such as a date ([Applitools match levels](https://applitools.com/docs/eyes/concepts/best-practices/match-levels)). sosie runs its filters as a list of named rules before comparison (drop attributes, mask text by selector or pattern, drop a subtree, round bounds) under the rule that "anything the tool ignores ... is stated in the config file, not buried in the implementation" ([sosie design document](https://github.com/cuihtlauac/sosie/blob/main/sosie-design.md)).

The research tools show both the value and the limits of filtering. CrossT ignored case, white space, attribute order and node text, took a list of patterns to exclude, and still had false positives on 12% to 37% of compared screens ([Mesbah and Prasad 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1985793.1985870)). The open reimplementation of X-PERT, archived since 2015, reports a change of sibling direction only when the gap between the two boxes also differs by more than 5 px, drops boxes of 5 px or less, and carries a list of 45 tag names to skip that includes SPAN, A and UL; a bug in the lookup means that list may have no effect there, and nothing confirms these constants are the paper's ([X-PERT AGDiff.java](https://github.com/orso-research-lab/xpert/blob/master/src/edu/gatech/xpert/dom/layout/AGDiff.java); [X-PERT AlignmentGraph.java](https://github.com/orso-research-lab/xpert/blob/master/src/edu/gatech/xpert/dom/layout/AlignmentGraph.java)). Dynamic content can be found without configuration by capturing the same version more than once: WebDiff took a second screenshot, and XBIDetective overlays five screenshots taken one second apart ([Roy Choudhary et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSM.2010.5609723); [Grewal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15804)). For a structural diff the same idea is cheap: elements whose box, text or style differ between two captures of one version are volatile and can be proposed as exclusions.

On approval, the evidence is thin. **No measurement was found of how often visual or snapshot baselines are approved without inspection.** A 2026 preprint compared 307 pull requests that had Chromatic results with 299 that had image attachments only: the first group took 3.8 times longer to resolve at the median and drew 10 times more discussion comments, with no significant difference in acceptance, and layout was the largest class of flagged issue, 39.7% of 189 ([Watanabe et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07020)). Jest's `--ci` flag refuses to store a new snapshot silently ([Jest CLI](https://jestjs.io/docs/cli)), and StyleProof's review mode needs approval from someone other than the author and reopens on a new push ([StyleProof README](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof)).

### Thirty-four longhands return values that depend on layout

`getComputedStyle` returns resolved values, and the CSSOM draft makes the resolved value the used value for sizes, margins, paddings and, on positioned elements, insets, but only when the property applies and the element has a box; colours and a non-normal `line-height` are used values too ([CSSOM, resolved values](https://drafts.csswg.org/cssom/#resolved-values)). Other specifications add cases: grid track lists resolve to used track sizes in px, a `transform` resolves to a matrix that includes resolved percentages, `transform-origin` and `perspective-origin` resolve to px, and border widths resolve to used values ([CSS Grid Level 2](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-grid-2/#resolved-track-list); [CSS Transforms Level 1](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-transforms-1/); [CSS Backgrounds Level 3](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-backgrounds-3/#border-width)). Both engines keep the list in source. Chromium flags **34 longhands** as `layout_dependent`: the four sizes, the eight insets, the eight margins, the eight paddings, the two grid track lists, `transform`, `transform-origin`, `perspective-origin` and `translate` ([Chromium css_properties.json5](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/css/css_properties.json5)). Its flag on `translate` conflicts with the Transforms Level 2 note that the resolved value of `translate` is the computed value, and which one Chrome follows was not tested. Gecko decides per property and per element: a margin given as a length, or a transform without a percentage, needs no layout ([Firefox nsComputedDOMStyle.cpp](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/layout/style/nsComputedDOMStyle.cpp)). The label therefore belongs to a value on an element, not to a property, and three labels fit the evidence: a used value that depends on layout, a used value that does not (colours, border widths), and a computed value. Only the first changes because an ancestor or sibling changed.

`computedStyleMap()` would separate the two cleanly, since it keeps `50%` and `auto`, but Firefox ships it only as a preview and its tracking bug is open ([CSS Typed OM Level 1](https://drafts.css-houdini.org/css-typed-om-1/#dom-element-computedstylemap); [browser-compat-data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Element.json); [Bugzilla 1278697](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1278697)). No research tool or product was found that reports one line per root cause for a computed-style diff. StyleProof comes nearest: its headline counts causes apart from differences, "1 distinct change (2 computed-style difference(s) · 3 state-delta difference(s))", and it prints properties that only follow `color` as child rows ([StyleProof README](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof)). The data needed for more folding is public. The W3C's webref package lists 822 properties with keys for inheritance, what a percentage refers to, logical property groups and legacy aliases; mdn/data is being retired in its favour ([w3c/webref CSS package](https://github.com/w3c/webref/blob/main/packages/css/README.md); [mdn/data](https://github.com/mdn/data)). Chrome's `CSS.getMatchedStylesForNode` returns the full cascade input for a node, and BiDi has no CSS module, so that route is Chrome-only ([CDP CSS domain](https://github.com/ChromeDevTools/devtools-protocol/blob/master/pdl/domains/CSS.pdl); [WebDriver BiDi](https://w3c.github.io/webdriver-bidi/)).

### What differs between Blink and Gecko before any page bug

The two engines do not expose the same properties. Chromium's property table has 484 longhands that appear on the computed style object, 42 of them behind runtime flags, and Gecko's defines 436 longhands, some limited to browser chrome or held behind preferences; about 385 names appear in both. These counts come from scripts run for this report over the two source files, not from release browsers, and are approximate ([Chromium css_properties.json5](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/css/css_properties.json5); [Firefox longhands.toml](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/servo/components/style/properties/longhands.toml)). A few names have a different status: `vertical-align` is a longhand in Chromium and a shorthand in Gecko, `border-spacing` the reverse, and Gecko's `font-width` is still `font-stretch` in Chromium's table. On values, the shared web-platform-tests show agreement on the main resolved-value cases in the stable runs of 11 November 2026 (insets, `line-height`, resolved colours) and a known set of differences: border widths on native checkboxes and radio buttons, `min-width: auto` with `aspect-ratio`, the resolved `width` of a scroll container, and insets on relatively positioned inlines ([wpt.fyi, css/cssom](https://wpt.fyi/results/css/cssom?label=master&label=stable)). In the wider set of computed-value tests, the engines also differ on colour serialisation, font serialisation and inline layout ([wpt.fyi, computed-value tests](https://wpt.fyi/results/css?label=master&label=stable&q=computed); pass counts compared for this report). The tests show where engines fail tests, not how often pages hit those cases.

Geometry differs for documented reasons. Blink lays out in units of 1/64 px and Gecko in units of 1/60 px ([Chromium layout_unit.h](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/geometry/layout_unit.h); [Firefox AppUnits.h](https://github.com/mozilla-firefox/firefox/blob/main/gfx/src/AppUnits.h)). CSS fixes no formula for `line-height: normal` and lets font fallback vary between browsers ([CSS Inline Layout Level 3](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-inline-3/#line-height-property); [CSS Fonts Level 4](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-fonts-4/)). Scrollbar type and size are left to the browser, native form controls carry themed paddings that no style sheet declares, and default styles are only "expected" ([CSS Overflow Level 3](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-overflow-3/#scrollbar-gutter-property); [HTML Standard, Rendering](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/rendering.html)). The browser vendors' own shared layout test helper accepts this: it **passes a measured value that is within 1 px of the expected one** ([WPT check-layout-th.js](https://github.com/web-platform-tests/wpt/blob/master/resources/check-layout-th.js)). No study was found that quantifies these differences on real pages, and none after X-PERT (2013) that samples real pages and counts layout differences between current engines. The Interop 2026 focus areas are newer features such as anchor positioning, scroll snap and view transitions, not flexbox, grid or positioning ([web.dev, Interop 2026](https://web.dev/blog/interop-2026)).

X-PERT is still the reference for comparing engines by relations. Its alignment graph holds containment with fill, justification and centring for parent and child, and strict direction plus edge alignment within 5 px for siblings ([Roy Choudhary et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE.2013.6606616); constants from the [reimplementation's Sibling.java](https://github.com/orso-research-lab/xpert/blob/master/src/edu/gatech/xpert/dom/layout/Sibling.java)). Its reported precision of 76% and recall of 95% could not be confirmed from the paper in this round. Later work moved away from relations and did not do better on layout. Classifiers over DOM and image features reached an F-measure of 0.70 on 42 responsive applications ([Watanabe et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3316808)) and F1 of 0.65 for external and 0.35 for internal layout failures on 72 ([Watanabe et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3580518)), both figures from abstracts. XBIDetective, which compares Firefox and Chrome screenshots with a fine-tuned vision-language model, reached 79% accuracy, 72% precision and 59% recall, missed font differences and invented spacing differences in five cases ([Grewal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15804)). No detector published between 2019 and 2026 that compares engines at the level of relations was found. onionskin's relation facts already contain X-PERT's graph: one Allen relation per axis gives its four directions and four edge alignments. Applitools gives the same advice for its own product, recommending the Layout level when one baseline serves several environments ([Applitools match levels](https://applitools.com/docs/eyes/concepts/best-practices/match-levels)).

### Two claims per verdict

The careful tools make two separate claims: what was compared came out equal, and this is the list of what was compared. StyleProof says it "certifies only what it captured", gives every surface a status (captured, excluded with reason, inaccessible, unknown, unproven determinism), never invents a coverage percentage, and can fail a strict run when a changed CSS rule matched no captured state ([StyleProof README](https://github.com/BenSheridanEdwards/StyleProof)). sosie calls its property whitelist "the trust boundary" and compares each engine against itself ([sosie design document](https://github.com/cuihtlauac/sosie/blob/main/sosie-design.md)). Playwright puts browser and platform in the baseline's file name ([Playwright snapshots guide](https://playwright.dev/docs/test-snapshots)). Both StyleProof and sosie are young projects without published user reports.

**What onionskin should take.** Filters as named rules in data, printed in every report: exclusion by selector that skips an element's own box and styles but still reports that it resized, text masks by pattern, and opt-in filters by kind of change. Propose volatile elements by capturing one version twice. Label each style value as layout-dependent, used without layout, or computed, per element, starting from Chromium's flag list checked against Gecko's code. Fold changes by cause: an inherited value at the highest changed element, followers of `color` under it, layout-dependent values under the element's box change, and moved boxes under the ancestor or earlier sibling that resized; print "N distinct changes (M differences)". Compare engines on relations among chosen elements with a tolerance just under 1 px, separate from the near-zero tolerance that suits one engine on one machine. Compare styles across engines only on a versioned whitelist of standard longhands with an explicit name table, and report properties outside it once per run. Warn when a page under cross-engine comparison uses no web font or leaves `line-height` at `normal`. End every result with the two claims. **What to avoid:** regions at fixed coordinates, a hard-coded list of tags to ignore, filters the user cannot see, an ignore rule that hides a resize, comparing resolved strings across engines, one tolerance for both uses, reading a text container's size difference as a layout bug without checking the font, an inferred cause shown as certain, and one green result that merges "equal" with "complete".

Several of those were one-line placeholders for the three directions studied here (numbers 5, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 and 17). ### Recommended order of work

All three directions are worth building, and the evidence gives an order. First comes groundwork that all three share: the parameter map as the capture key with a record of what was not captured (21, 24), identity fields (41), and text boxes with scroll sizes (35). Each is a small addition to the capture script and data, and none changes existing behaviour.

Second comes element matching (42 to 45). It repairs a feature that already ships, it is the smallest self-contained piece (the algorithm is a few hundred lines and can be tested without a browser), and the evidence against the current method is the most consistent in this report. Every later diff depends on it, including diffs between width ranges, between a resting and a stressed capture, and between engines. Value labels, cause folding and named filters (46 to 48) follow directly, since they are pure functions over paired captures.

Third comes states and preferences (22, 23, 25 to 28), Chrome first. The protocol calls exist, in-page capture stays as it is, and coverage reporting makes the gain measurable. Firefox gets real input and colour scheme at once and the other preferences by relaunch. The whole CSS domain being experimental is the main risk, which is why a failed call must surface as "not captured".

Fourth comes width ranges (29 to 34). This is the largest build, it has the strongest quantitative evidence, and no maintained tool offers it. It also has to exist before the next step, because the best intent filter for spec-free checks is the comparison across widths.

Fifth comes the three default spec-free checks (36 to 40). Without the cross-width and stress comparisons they would repeat ReDeCheck's record, in which 40% of reports were not true positives. With them, and limited to text and the viewport, they rest on the parts of the evidence that held up. Wrapping, small-range hints and child-outside-parent stay opt-in and informational.

Last comes comparison across engines (49 to 52). It needs matching, labels and the whitelist, its tolerance and exclusions have the thinnest evidence, and there is no study of how often real pages differ between current engines. It should start as relation comparison among elements a developer has named.

## Conclusion

The three directions turn out to be one mechanism seen from three sides. Every result that held up in the literature compares a page with itself under one changed parameter: the next wider viewport (ReDeCheck), a larger text size (AccessiText, dVermin), another language (GWALI), another engine (X-PERT), or the previous version. The intent filter for spec-free checks is such a comparison, a width range is a compact record of many of them, and a forced state is one more variant. What all of them need is a dependable way to say that an element in one capture is the same element in another, which is why matching comes first, and a capture key that states exactly which parameters differ, which is why the parameter map comes with it. Seen this way, onionskin does not need a separate checker beside its diff. It needs a diff that can be pointed at any two captures and told which differences to expect.

The largest unknowns are again empirical, and onionskin can produce some of the missing evidence from its own use. Applying known mutations (wrap, unwrap, move, duplicate) to captured trees of real pages would give the first accuracy figures for subtree matching on DOM trees. A small labelled set of real overlaps, run with each intent filter on and off, would be the controlled comparison that no paper has made. Recording how often a finding at one width disappears at the next, and how often Chrome and Firefox disagree on a relation at a 1 px tolerance on pages with pinned fonts, would put numbers where the published work has constants. The Firefox gap is the one constraint likely to move by itself, since the bug for the remaining media features is open, so the design should detect features at run time and not fix today's limits into its interface.
